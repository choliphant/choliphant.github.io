§18.4 · pp. 610–611 / PDF pp. 640–641

Random forests vary the split search at each node.

  1. Give each tree a randomized training sample. In the standard bootstrap version, draw training rows with replacement for each tree.
  2. At each node, draw a fresh subset Si of the D input features. Search those features and their candidate thresholds for the best split on the cases reaching that node.
  3. Combine the fitted trees. Average regression predictions. For classification, the aggregation can be a vote or an average of class probabilities followed by a class decision, depending on the implementation.

If a strong predictor appears at almost every root, bagged trees may reuse its split and make similar errors. Restricting the candidate set gives other features a chance. A smaller set can lower error correlation and can also weaken individual splits when it hides useful features. With all D features eligible, the extra feature restriction disappears and the otherwise matched bootstrap-tree procedure becomes ordinary tree bagging. Each tree can be trained in parallel with the others.

Feature eligibility · original example

With D = 6 and k = 2, exactly 5 of the 15 equally likely feature pairs include a particular feature. It is eligible with probability 2/6. If two of the six features are useful at this node, the chance of seeing at least one is 1 − C(4,2)/C(6,2) = 9/15 = .60. More generally, for K useful features, replace 4 with D − K and 2 with k in the numerator. A combination count is zero when there are too few features to fill the subset.

Why diversity helps · explanatory model

For M prediction errors of equal variance σ² and common pairwise correlation ρ, averaging gives variance σ²[ρ + (1−ρ)/M]. This follows by summing M variances and M(M−1) off-diagonal covariances, then dividing by M². At M = 100 and ρ = .20, the ratio to one error variance is .208. At ρ = .50 it is .505. This model isolates the effect of correlation while holding each learner’s variance fixed.

Read the figures as individual experiments. Figure 18.4 shows a 50-tree forest and a 50-tree bagging ensemble on one two-dimensional dataset, with test accuracies .90 and .91 respectively. Figure 18.5 compares test error versus number of trees for 4,601 labeled emails. The 57 inputs comprise 48 word-frequency features, 6 character-frequency features, and 3 summaries of capital-letter runs. The forest curve improves on bagging in that comparison, and the boosting curve attains lower test error still. Useful split alternatives and lower error correlation are the motivation for the forest design. Neither figure establishes a general ranking or isolates the causal effect of irrelevant features.

§18.5 introduction and §18.5.1 · pp. 611–612 / PDF pp. 641–642

Add one fitted correction and freeze it.

An additive model sums fitted functions. Each Fm can be a tree or another function approximator. The model is linear in its component outputs, which can themselves be nonlinear functions of x. For forward stagewise fitting, start from a specified f0, then optimize only the next learner and its step size. The loss below measures training fit. The book also allows adding a regularization term.

L(f) = ∑i ℓ(yi, f(xi)) · Eq. 18.16
(βm, θm) = argminβ,θ ∑i ℓ(yi, fm−1(xi) + βF(xi; θ)) · Eq. 18.17
fm(x) = fm−1(x) + βmFm(x) · Eq. 18.18

The earlier learners keep their fitted parameters and weights. The next one is trained with their combined prediction already in place, so changing an earlier stage can change every later stage. With the appropriate weak-learning conditions, successive better-than-chance binary learners can drive down ensemble training error. The condition concerns current weighted error, and lower training loss alone gives no guarantee about test error.

Boosting can improve systematic fit through sequential correction, while randomized averaging primarily targets variance. Those are tendencies. Both bias and variance can change when the learner or its settings change. The number of stages M is a complexity setting. Use a validation curve to choose a stopping point and keep final test data separate.

§18.5.2 · pp. 612–613 / PDF pp. 642–643

Fit a tree to the residuals and add its predictions.

At stage m, define rim = yi − fm−1(xi). The stage objective for squared loss becomes a regression problem with r as the target.

[yi − (fm−1(xi) + βF(xi))]² = [rim − βF(xi)]² · Eq. 18.19

Regression trees have freely fitted leaf values, so their scale can absorb β. Set β = 1 and fit F by least squares to r. Each leaf predicts its mean residual. Then add that leaf correction to the old ensemble prediction and recompute every residual. Within each new leaf, the updated residuals have mean zero with a full step. Individual residuals can remain, and further stages can split the cases differently.

Original worked round. At x = {1, 2, 3, 4}, let y = {1, 3, 7, 9}. The initial constant f0 = 5 minimizes squared loss, giving r = {−4, −2, 2, 4}. The best stump splits at 2.5 with leaf means −3 and +3. Thus f1 = {2, 2, 8, 8} and the new residuals are {−1, 1, −1, 1}. Mean squared training error falls from 10 to 1. At x = 2, the tree outputs −3 and the ensemble outputs 2.

The lab also lets you shrink each fitted correction by a factor η between 0 and 1. For the worked round with η = .5, predictions would be {3.5, 3.5, 6.5, 6.5} with MSE 3.25. This is a learning-rate extension to Eq. 18.19’s full-step fit. Changing η changes the residuals used in future fits.

§18.5.3 · pp. 613–614 / PDF pp. 643–644

Two losses react differently to the same mistake.

Use labels ỹ ∈ {−1,+1} and a real score f(x). The sign predicts the class, with a tie rule needed at zero. The signed margin u = ỹf(x) is positive for a correct sign and negative for an incorrect one. When f represents half the log odds, π(x) = P(y = +1|x) = σ(2f(x)), where σ(a) = 1/(1 + e−a). This link concerns the real score, while discrete AdaBoost’s individual voters return only ±1.

Exponential loss ℓexp(u) = e−u · Eq. 18.22
Log loss ℓlog(u) = ln(1 + e−2u) · Eq. 18.21, in nats

When u is very negative, log loss is approximately −2u with a slope magnitude bounded by 2. Exponential loss grows as e|u|, and its slope magnitude grows at the same rate. For u = −4, the losses are approximately 54.598 and 8.0003. A badly misclassified point, including a possible label error, can therefore exert much greater influence under exponential loss.

Population link. Fix x and let p be its true positive-class probability. The expected exponential loss is p e−f + (1−p)ef. Setting its derivative to zero gives e2f* = p/(1−p). Thus both exponential loss and log loss have the same unrestricted population minimizer f* = ½ ln[p/(1−p)] for 0 < p < 1. At p = 0 or 1 the optimum is approached as f tends to the corresponding infinity. For p = .8, f* = ½ ln 4 ≈ .6931. The population argument does not establish that a finite-stage, finite-data AdaBoost score is calibrated.

Figure 18.7 uses base 2 for its log-loss curve. Divide the natural-log curve by ln 2 to get that scale. The base-2 loss is at least 1 when u ≤ 0, so it upper-bounds the 0–1 loss under either convention at a zero margin. Natural-log loss at u = 0 is ln 2 < 1. Exponential loss is already at least 1 for u ≤ 0.

§18.5.3, Eqs. 18.20–18.25 and Figure 18.7. See also Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani (2000), §4.2, for the population argument.

§18.5.3 · pp. 614–616 / PDF pp. 644–646

AdaBoost changes who matters in the next classification fit.

Let h = Fm be a hard ±1 classifier. Expanding the stage loss shows that the existing ensemble gives each case a weight proportional to e−ỹᵢfₘ₋₁(xᵢ). Let W be total weight and ε the weighted fraction of cases h misclassifies. Then Lm = W[(1−ε)e−β + εeβ]. For a positive step β, a smaller ε lowers Lm, so first fit a classifier to minimize weighted mistakes. Differentiating in β gives the second step.

ε = [∑i ωi I(ỹi ≠ h(xi))] / ∑i ωi
β = ½ ln[(1−ε)/ε],   fm = fm−1 + βh
ω′i ∝ ωi e−βỹᵢh(xᵢ) · Eqs. 18.26–18.35

A correct prediction multiplies its raw weight by e−β. A mistake multiplies it by eβ. Multiplying all new weights by the common factor eβ gives the equivalent update in Algorithm 18.1. Leave correct weights as they were, multiply mistakes by eα, and use α = 2β. Normalizing afterwards is optional for the weighted error formula, which already divides by total weight. The sign of ∑αmhm equals the sign of ∑βmhm. The two raw scores differ by a factor of two.

Original worked round. Six cases start at weight 1/6. If the stump misses only case 5, ε = 1/6, β = ½ ln 5 ≈ .8047, and α = ln 5. In the α convention the five correct cases keep raw weight 1/6 while case 5 gets 5/6. After normalization, each correct case has weight .10 and case 5 has .50. Under an exact full step, total weight on the cases missed by the current stump becomes .50 and the other .50 sits on cases that stump got right. Later stumps train on this new distribution.

At ε = .5, the coefficient is zero. A worse-than-chance learner has a negative coefficient under the unconstrained formula. If the hypothesis family includes its flipped predictions, use the flipped learner and error 1−ε to retain a positive step. At ε = 0 the unregularized optimum is an infinite step, so the finite formula needs an edge-case rule such as stopping. A lower weighted error below .5 gives a stronger vote. The book also mentions Real AdaBoost for probability-valued base learners and SAMME as a multiclass extension.

§18.5.3, Eqs. 18.26–18.35 and Algorithm 18.1. The finite-step weight balance follows by substituting the optimal β into the two loss terms.

§18.5.4 · pp. 616–617 / PDF pp. 646–647

LogitBoost fits a Newton step by weighted regression.

Use y* ∈ {0,1} with y* = (ỹ+1)/2. Start with f0 = 0 and πi = .5. Let g = 2f be the log-odds score, so πi = σ(g(xi)). The first two derivatives of Bernoulli negative log likelihood with respect to g are πi − y*i and πi(1−πi). The local quadratic objective therefore gives this update.

  1. Compute curvature weights wi = πi(1−πi) and working responses zi = (y*i − πi)/wi.
  2. Fit a real-valued learner Fm by minimizing ∑i wi[zi − F(xi)]². This estimates the increment in g. In a tree leaf, the weighted mean is ∑ wizi/∑ wi = ∑(y*i − πi)/∑ wi, with sums restricted to that leaf.
  3. Update f ← f + ½Fm (equivalently g ← g + Fm). Recompute π = σ(2f) before the next round. Threshold π at .5 or take the sign of f for the hard decision. The book also points to a multiclass generalization.

One leaf · original calculation

With three positive cases and one negative case, all at π = .5, the weights are .25 and z = {2, 2, 2, −2}. The constant correction is F = 1. A full Newton step gives f = .5 and π = σ(1) ≈ .7311.

A confident mistake

For y* = 1 and π = .01, w = .0099 and z = 100, but w·z = .99. A small curvature weight can accompany a large working response. In a leaf the denominator ∑w controls the Newton step. Very small curvature can make it large, and the local quadratic approximation can be poor far from the current score.

LogitBoost uses a Bernoulli likelihood, so σ(2f) is its modeled probability. The book’s warning about recovering probabilities with exponential loss refers to the absence of that likelihood. The population mapping in Eq. 18.25 still exists. Applying that mapping to a fitted AdaBoost half-score can produce a probability estimate, whose calibration needs separate evidence.
