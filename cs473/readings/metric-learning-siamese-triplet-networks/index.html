Start here · the connection

A neighbor is only as useful as your notion of distance.

  1. Store examplesKNN compares a query with labeled examples in a gallery. The gallery can grow as new classes arrive.
  2. Learn a representationA neural network maps raw inputs into vectors. Train the representation so relevant similarity appears in the geometry.
  3. Compare embeddingsUse distance for retrieval, nearest neighbor classification, or verification. Siamese and triplet training teach the representation how to compare.

Running example: two photos of the same person should be similar despite lighting or pose; two different people should be distinguishable. “Positive” means a matching example, not a positive coordinate or a class called “positive.” The application determines what should count as a match.

16.1 & 16.1.1 · K nearest neighbors

Let nearby examples vote.

Store the labeled training examples. For a new query x, find its K nearest examples and estimate each class probability by its fraction of the neighborhood.

p(y = c | x, D) = (1/K) ∑n ∈ NK(x,D) I(yn = c)
Eq. 16.1 · I is 1 when its condition is true, otherwise 0.

Original practice: with labels [A, B, A, A, B], K = 5 gives p(A | x) = 3/5 = 0.6. These are neighborhood vote fractions, not a guarantee that 60% of all such predictions will be right.

The two key choices are K and the distance metric. With K = 1, each training example owns a Voronoi cell. With distinct training locations, counting each point itself yields zero 1-NN training error; this does not imply good test accuracy.

Small K can fit noise; larger K smooths decisions and may underfit. Choose K using validation data. K is a neighbor count, not the number of classes.

16.1.2 & 16.1.3 · Limits of raw neighbors

“Nearest” may still be far away.

For uniform data in a unit D-dimensional cube, a cube covering volume fraction p has side length s satisfying sD = p. Hence:

s = p1/D
p = 0.10, D = 10 → s ≈ 0.794
p = 0.01, D = 10 → s ≈ 0.631

This idealized volume argument explains why local estimation becomes difficult in high dimension. A useful representation can discard irrelevant variation; it does not guarantee that all high-dimensional problems become easy.

Storage and search: KNN retains its examples. A brute-force scan evaluates distances to all N examples (roughly O(ND) coordinate work for D-dimensional Euclidean vectors), plus neighbor selection. Pruning can reduce storage; partitioning structures, hashing, and approximate nearest neighbor search can reduce search work, with tradeoffs.

The book discusses k-d trees, clustering based indexes, LSH, and FAISS. There is no universal dimension at which every exact search method stops working well; efficiency depends on the data and index.

16.1.4 · Open set recognition

A nearest match is not automatically a valid match.

A fixed classifier must choose from its known classes unless it has a rejection mechanism. In an open world, the query may belong to an unseen category. Compare the query with a gallery, then decide whether the best match is sufficiently similar.

Distinguish these situations

  • Known identity: another image of a person already in the gallery.
  • Novel class: a new person who is absent from the gallery.
  • OOD input: an unrelated input, such as a dog image arriving at a face system.
  • Incremental learning: after obtaining a new label, add examples and expand the known class set.

What a useful embedding enables

Verification compares two examples; identification searches a gallery. Few-shot classification uses only a few labeled examples for a new class. A learned embedding can be reused while the gallery grows.

A distance threshold is a separate decision rule. The training margin is not automatically the right verification threshold. Rejecting a match does not by itself establish whether the cause is a new class or OOD input.

Other open world applications include entity resolution (do two names refer to the same entity?) and multi-object tracking (is this observation an existing tracked object or a new one?). Both need a meaningful matching rule.

16.2.2 · Deep metric learning

The network outputs a vector, not just a class.

e = f(x; θ) ∈ ℝL,   ê = e / ‖e‖₂   (e ≠ 0)
rij = ‖êi − êj‖₂
qij = rij² = 2 − 2sij,   sij = êiTêj
Eqs. 16.7–16.9 · Smaller r or q means closer; larger cosine similarity s means more similar.

Normalization places nonzero embeddings on the unit hypersphere. Then cosine similarity lies in [−1, 1], Euclidean distance r in [0, 2], and squared distance q in [0, 4]. A zero embedding needs explicit numerical handling before normalization.

Original practice: a 60° angle

For unit vectors a = (1, 0) and b = (1/2, √3/2), s = 1/2. Thus q = 2 − 2(1/2) = 1 and r = 1. The ordering by largest cosine equals the ordering by smallest normalized Euclidean distance.

What supervision is needed?

Class labels can define matching pairs, but known pair relationships can also provide supervision. The network learns the aspects that those relationships reward. It does not discover a universal definition of semantic similarity.

Notation guide for the losses: this companion always uses r for Euclidean distance and q = r² for squared distance. Eq. 16.7 writes a squared distance as d, while Eqs. 16.10–16.11 also contain d². To avoid silently squaring twice, the loss examples below explicitly use the conventional Euclidean r and squared q forms. If you instead choose squared distance as the base d in those equations, you obtain a different numerical objective; do not mix the conventions.

16.2.3 · Classification losses

Correct classes do not guarantee useful distances.

One simple method is to train a classifier and reuse a hidden layer, commonly the penultimate layer, as an embedding. This is scalable and uses ordinary class supervision. But being on the correct side of a decision boundary does not force same-class examples to be close or different-class examples to be far apart.

Original thought experiment: a classifier uses only the first coordinate to separate two classes. Points (1, 100) and (1, −100) can both be confidently classified as A while remaining 200 units apart. Classification can succeed while within-class geometry is poor.

Ranking losses train the relationships more directly. They can use “these match / these do not” supervision without requiring a named class for every example. This still requires a justified way to create those relationships.

16.2.4.1 · Pairwise contrastive loss

One encoder. Two uses.

A Siamese network applies the same feature extractor to two inputs. The two branches share θ: they are two evaluations of one learned function. This is more than using the same architecture or starting from identical weights.

Compare the two outputs, compute one loss, then backpropagate through both uses of the encoder. Their gradient contributions accumulate into the shared parameters.

t = 1 for a similar pair; t = 0 for a dissimilar pair
Lpair = t r² + (1 − t) [max(0, m − r)]²,   m > 0
Eq. 16.10 in the explicit r = Euclidean-distance convention used throughout this guide; no extra factor of ½.

Similar pair: pull together

L = r². With r = 0.7, the loss is 0.49. Positive pairs keep paying until their distance is zero in this objective; there is no positive-pair safety margin here.

For r > 0, ∂L/∂r = 2r. Minimizing the loss favors a smaller distance.

Dissimilar pair: separate enough

L = max(0, m − r)². With m = 1 and r = 0.7, the loss is 0.09. With r = 1.2, the loss is zero. “Push apart” stops once the safety margin is reached.

For r < m, ∂L/∂r = −2(m − r); for r ≥ m it is zero. These are distance derivatives; encoder gradients also require the chain rule.

Avoid collapse: pulling only positive pairs together permits every input to get the same embedding. Dissimilar pairs penalize that collapsed solution. The loss alone does not guarantee that an optimizer will find a good representation.

Training vs. use: training needs paired inputs and match information to compute the loss. Later, encode each gallery example once and encode a query with the same f. Retrieval or verification compares the vectors; it need not rerun a separate neural classifier for every pair.

Naively enumerating all training pairs scales as O(N²). The Siamese architecture describes shared branches; contrastive loss is the particular objective here. The terms are related, but not synonyms for every possible architecture or loss.

16.2.4.2 · Triplet loss

One anchor. Two comparisons.

A triplet contains an anchor a, a positive p that should match the anchor, and a negative n that should not. All three pass through the same encoder. The loss couples the two anchor distances in one comparison.

a → f( · ; θ) → êa
p → f( · ; θ) → êp
n → f( · ; θ) → ên

Three uses of the same θ, not three independently trained models.

The anchor is the reference for both distances. The loss below does not directly contain the positive–negative distance. A negative is simply dissimilar to this anchor; it need not be dissimilar to every other negative.

qap = ‖êa − êp‖₂²,   qan = ‖êa − ên‖₂²
Ltriplet = max(0, qap − qan + m)
L = 0 ⇔ qan ≥ qap + m
Eq. 16.11 with d treated as Euclidean distance, so d² is q. This margin is in squared-distance units.

Correct order, insufficient margin

Let qap = 0.4, qan = 0.6, and m = 0.5. The positive is closer, but L = max(0, 0.4 − 0.6 + 0.5) = 0.3. The required negative distance is at least 0.9.

Zero loss at qan = 0.9 does not require moving the positive all the way onto the anchor.

What learning tries to do

Inside the active hinge, ∂L/∂qap = +1 and ∂L/∂qan = −1. Minimization favors reducing the positive distance and/or increasing the negative distance. Outside the hinge, this triplet contributes no gradient; at the boundary a subgradient convention is used.

Actual movement depends on the shared encoder, normalization, and the other triplets. A loss plot is not a simulation of full network training.

Why unit normalization matters to the margin

Because q = 2 − 2s, the zero-loss condition is equivalent to sap − san ≥ m/2. With m = 0.5, the required cosine gap is 0.25. For unit vectors q lies in [0, 4], so a margin above 4 is impossible even for an ideal single triplet; a particular dataset may make much smaller margins infeasible too.

Pairwise vs. triplet: contrastive training asks “is this pair close, or at least m apart?” Triplet training asks “is the positive closer than the negative by at least m in squared distance?” A triplet can have zero loss while its positive distance remains nonzero. A collapsed embedding has triplet loss m, so collapse is not a zero-loss solution.

All possible triples give a naive O(N³) enumeration. In practice the book uses minibatches containing a positive and a negative for an anchor. Each triplet still compares just one negative. Detailed sampling and mining techniques in §16.2.5 are outside your assigned scope.

16.2.4.3 · N-pairs loss

Ask the positive to win against several negatives.

Triplet loss sees one negative at a time. N-pairs compares one anchor with one positive and N − 1 negatives. Here N counts candidates in this loss, not necessarily the full training dataset size.

s+ = êaTêp,   sk− = êaTênₖ
L = log[1 + ∑k=1N−1 exp(sk− − s+)]
= −log[exp(s+) / (exp(s+) + ∑k exp(sk−))]
Eqs. 16.12–16.13 · Softmax cross-entropy with the positive candidate as the target.

Original practice: all N candidates have the same similarity. Their softmax probabilities are 1/N, so L = log N. With two candidates, L = log 2 ≈ 0.693; with four, L = log 4 ≈ 1.386.

With N = 2, L = log(1 + exp(s− − s+)), the logistic / softplus form in Eq. 16.14. It is smooth and remains positive for finite scores. Contrast this with the similarity hinge max(0, s− − s+ + 1) in Eq. 16.15, which has a flat zero region. Do not transfer a numerical margin unchanged between cosine and squared-distance forms.

The book connects N-pairs to InfoNCE and describes NT-Xent as a temperature-scaled form. With temperature τ, similarities enter as s/τ. Lower τ sharpens the softmax; τ = 1 gives the formula above. These candidate probabilities are not automatically calibrated probabilities that two inputs represent the same identity.
